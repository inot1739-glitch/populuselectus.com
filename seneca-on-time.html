What Would Seneca Say About Your Relationship With Time?

He wrote the most powerful essay on time 2,000 years ago. It's a more accurate diagnosis of modern life than most books written this decade.

Around 49 AD, the Roman philosopher Lucius Annaeus Seneca wrote a short essay called De Brevitate Vitae — On the Shortness of Life. It opens with one of the most striking sentences in the history of philosophy:

"It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a great deal of it." — Seneca, On the Shortness of Life

Two thousand years later, the diagnosis is more accurate than ever. We have more leisure time than any generation in history — and we feel more rushed, more distracted, and more behind than ever. Seneca understood why. And he had a solution.

Who Was Seneca?

Seneca was a Stoic philosopher, playwright, and advisor to Emperor Nero — making him simultaneously one of the most principled and one of the most compromised figures in ancient Rome. He wrote extensively about ethics, time, friendship, and death, often while navigating the impossible contradictions of life at the imperial court. Unlike Marcus Aurelius, who applied Stoicism from supreme power, and Epictetus, who applied it from slavery, Seneca applied it from the morally murky middle — which is exactly where most of us live.

The Central Argument: Life Is Long Enough

Seneca's core claim is counterintuitive: life is not too short. It's long enough — more than long enough — to live fully and accomplish everything that genuinely matters. The problem is not the quantity of time we're given. It's the quality of our relationship with it.

We don't live long enough. We die long before our bodies fail — we die the moment we stop actually inhabiting our lives and start letting them happen to us. The person scrolling their phone for three hours isn't living for those three hours. They're just passing through time.

This distinction — between time lived and time passed — is Seneca's central contribution. And it maps perfectly onto modern life in ways he couldn't have imagined.

The 5 Ways We Waste Our Lives

1. We give our time to other people's priorities. Seneca was withering about people who spent their lives chasing political favor, social approval, and others' agendas. "People are frugal in guarding their personal property; but as soon as it comes to squandering time they are most wasteful of the one thing in which it is right to be stingy." We guard our money carefully. We give away our time freely. The ratio is backwards.
2. We defer our real life to "later." "Everyone hustles his life along, and is troubled by a longing for the future and weariness of the present." Seneca saw people spending their entire lives getting ready to live — saving money, building security, waiting for the right moment. The right moment is always today. The person who says "I'll travel when I retire" often doesn't travel when they retire.
3. We confuse busyness with purpose. Being busy is not the same as being alive. Seneca described busy people as having the appearance of productivity while never actually engaging with what they're doing. The executive who works 80-hour weeks on things that don't matter is less alive, in Seneca's terms, than someone who spends an hour in genuine conversation. This connects directly to eliminating the habits that kill real productivity.
4. We fear death instead of fearing unlived life. Most people's relationship with time is shaped by fear of death — the end of time. Seneca thought this was the wrong fear. The real tragedy isn't dying; it's reaching death having never truly lived. "It is not death that a man should fear, but he should fear never beginning to live."
5. We forget that the past is the only time we truly own. The future is uncertain. The present is fleeting. But the past — what you have genuinely experienced and done — is permanent and inalienable. Seneca counseled actively reclaiming the past through memory and reflection, treating it as a reservoir of life already lived rather than a place of regret.

How to Apply Seneca Today

Three practices directly from Seneca's philosophy:

The time audit. For one week, track how you spend every hour. Not to judge it — to see it clearly. Seneca believed most people had never actually looked at where their time went. The act of seeing it, honestly, is usually enough to begin changing it.
The deathbed question. At the end of each day: "If this were my last day, would I be satisfied with how I used it?" Not every day needs to be extraordinary. But no day should be entirely squandered on things that leave no trace in memory or meaning.
Protect a daily hour for yourself. Seneca wrote: "Vindica te tibi" — reclaim yourself for yourself. Before you give your time to your job, your family, your social media — protect one hour per day that belongs entirely to you. Reading, thinking, creating, walking. The content matters less than the ownership.

Combined with the discipline of a daily routine and the habits of self-discipline, Seneca's philosophy of time offers a complete framework for living intentionally rather than accidentally.

Key Takeaways

Seneca's argument: life isn't too short. We waste it — and we've been wasting it in the same ways for 2,000 years.

  • Life is long enough — the problem is how we relate to time, not how much we have
  • We give time freely to others while guarding money carefully — the ratio is backwards
  • Busyness is not aliveness — many busy people are the least alive
  • The real fear shouldn't be death; it should be never beginning to live
  • The past is the only time you truly own — reclaim it through reflection
  • Do a time audit for one week — see clearly where your life actually goes
  • Protect one daily hour that belongs entirely to you
